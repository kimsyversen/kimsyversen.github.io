Threat modeling

Think like an attacker while changes are still cheap. Four questions and one drawing take you from idea to a short list of fixes. The example all the way through is a made-up claims portal.

Question 2

STRIDE: six ways to fail

  • SSpoofing. Are you who you say you are?
  • TTampering. Can someone change data unseen?
  • RRepudiation. Can someone deny what they did?
  • IInformation disclosure. Can the wrong person read it?
  • DDenial of service. Can someone stop the service?
  • EElevation of privilege. Can someone do more than they should?

Question 3

Score and rank

Score = likelihood × impact

Rate each from 1 to 5, then multiply. You get 1 to 25.

Suggested levels

  • High, 15 or more. Fix before launch.
  • Middle, 8 to 14. Put in the plan, with a date.
  • Low, 7 or less. Accept or watch, in writing.
Why not DREAD?

DREAD gives five numbers per threat: damage, reproducibility, exploitability, affected users and discoverability. Use two numbers instead. They fit on a plain risk matrix. Scores are a tool for discussion, not a calculation that is right.

Question 3

Pick one of four responses

Remove

Take away the feature or the data that creates the threat.

EThe portal never opens uploaded files.

Reduce

Add a control that makes it less likely or less harmful.

IAn ownership check on every fetch.

Move

Hand the responsibility to someone else, with a ready-made service and a written agreement.

Sign-inAn external provider checks passwords, so the portal never receives them.

Accept

Only in writing, with a named owner and a date to look again.

RProduct owner, review in six months.

Question 3

Make controls provable (threat I)

Question 4

Good enough? Check, then repeat

  • Did we miss something? Walk the drawing again with people who were not in the room. Is there another way to the data, or more people with access?
  • Do the controls work? Run the tests above. Add security testing and code review where you can.
  • What did we learn? Write down what was hard or overlooked, and take it into the next round.

Run it again when

  • You add an integration, a new kind of data or a new trust boundary.
  • Something goes wrong, or you hear of an attack on something similar.
  • An accepted risk reaches its review date.

Done when

  • Scope, assets and drawing are written down.
  • Every threat is one sentence with a score and a level.
  • Every threat has one of the four responses and an owner.
  • Every control has a test, and the test has run.
  • You know when the model comes up again.

Question 1 · The fictional claims portal

Map the system

What are we protecting, and which flows cross into the cloud?

  • Assets. Customer documents, identities and uptime.
  • Crossings. Browser traffic and the sign-in provider’s identity response. Passwords go to the provider.
  • Scope. Sign-in, upload and viewing. Leave the handlers’ internal system for a separate model.

Question 3 · Prioritization

Score and rank the risks

  • IInformation disclosure: a customer reads another customer’s documents.
  • RRepudiation: a customer denies sending or changing a document.

Which comes first, document disclosure (4 × 5) or an unlogged upload (2 × 3)?

4 × 5 = 20

I · High. Fix document access first. R · Low (2 × 3 = 6). Record who accepts the risk and when they’ll review it.

Question 3 · Treatment

Pick a response and test it

  • Remove. Take away the feature or data that creates the threat.
  • Reduce. Lower the likelihood or impact.
  • Move. Transfer responsibility through an agreement.
  • Accept. Record who owns the risk and when they’ll review it.

How could we keep document viewing and prove that customers only see their own files?

Reduce. The server checks ownership on every document request. The developer owns the check.

Test. Use two test customers. Confirm each can open their own document and that the server refuses access to the other customer’s document.

Question 4 · Verification

Review and repeat

  • Check coverage. Walk the diagram with someone who wasn’t in the session.
  • Check evidence. Run the tests before revising the score.
  • Revisit decisions. Review accepted risks on their due date and update the model when the system changes.

Claims handlers now need direct portal access. What would you change in the model?

  • Add the handler, the new flow and any trust boundary it crosses.
  • Decide whether handlers may open claims assigned to someone else.
  • Give the access rule an owner. Test allowed and denied access, then reassess the risk.
